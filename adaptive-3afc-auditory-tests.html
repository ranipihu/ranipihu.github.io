Adaptive 3AFC auditory tests

Measure a just-detectable difference with three alternatives per trial: two identical references and one subtly different target. Complete each test independently; the procedure ends at six reversals.

Listening safety: run each test in a quiet environment and start with a low computer volume. Do not use uncomfortable or potentially harmful sound levels; stop immediately if any sound is uncomfortable. This educational tool is not a clinical hearing assessment.

Why this matters beyond the lab

  • Auditory icons and notifications: a pitch difference must clear the listener’s discrimination threshold to make app events distinct without becoming intrusive.
  • Alarms: reliably distinguishable pitch patterns can communicate urgency, source, or action while reducing confusable alerts.
  • Music perception: pitch discrimination supports melody, tuning, harmony, intonation, and recognition of an instrument or voice.
  • Human–machine communication: calibrated differences help machines convey state, progress, and errors through sound when vision is busy or unavailable.
  • Volume control and accessibility: amplitude discrimination informs usable volume increments and helps ensure speech, media, and alerts remain distinguishable for listeners with different hearing needs.
  • Warnings and workplace sound: audibly distinct levels can separate routine cues from urgent warnings, while careful calibration avoids masking, distraction, and unsafe exposure.

Procedure notes: adaptive staircases place observations near threshold and use shrinking step sizes; forced-choice tasks have a chance baseline determined by their number of alternatives. See Purdue adaptive-methods notes, 2-down/1-up background, and Levitt’s transformed up-down methods.
